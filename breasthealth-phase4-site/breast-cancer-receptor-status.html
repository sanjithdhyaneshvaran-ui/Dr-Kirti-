Receptor Status: Understanding Your Biopsy Report

4 min read · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kirti

Your biopsy report includes tests that show what fuels the cancer's growth. These results — called receptor status — are some of the most important for choosing your treatment. This page explains what ER, PR and HER2 mean, in plain words.

Hormone receptors: ER and PR

Some breast cancer cells have that let the hormones and progesterone fuel their growth. If they do, the cancer is called hormone receptor-positive (ER-positive and/or PR-positive). This is the most common kind.

The good news is that these cancers usually respond well to hormone therapy (endocrine therapy), which blocks the hormones' effect.

HER2

is a protein that helps cells grow. Some cancers have too much of it; these are called HER2-positive. They can grow faster, but there are very effective that attack HER2 directly.

The three main subtypes

Hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative

What it means
The most common subtype; often slower growing
Treatments often used
Surgery, hormone therapy, sometimes chemotherapy and radiotherapy

HER2-positive

What it means
More HER2 protein; can grow faster, but responds well to targeted therapy
Treatments often used
Surgery, targeted therapy with chemotherapy, sometimes hormone therapy

Triple negative

What it means
No ER, PR or HER2; more common in younger women and in women with a BRCA1 gene change
Treatments often used
Surgery and chemotherapy; newer treatments such as immunotherapy in some cases

If you have triple-negative breast cancer, or you were diagnosed at a young age, Dr. Kirti may suggest genetic testing.
